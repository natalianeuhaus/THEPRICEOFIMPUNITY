ABOUT THE INVESTIGATION

The Price of Gold examines gold mining, deforestation, sex trafficking, and the institutions responsible for enforcing the law in Madre de Dios. Its objective is to establish what can be documented, identify what remains unknown, and investigate where the evidence converges—without assuming that overlapping activities were coordinated.

The investigation asks whether the persistence of illegal mining and human exploitation can be explained solely by the challenges of governing a remote region, or whether abuses of power and failures of oversight have helped sustain them. It draws on satellite imagery, judicial records, prosecutorial investigations, government audits, and independent reporting to trace the documented relationships between economic incentives, environmental destruction, exploitation, and institutional decisions.

To examine those institutional conditions, the project uses Robert Klitgaard's analytical framework, C = M + D − A:

C — Corruption: the abuse of entrusted power for private gain.

M — Monopoly power: concentrated control over gold-bearing territory, extraction, trade, or regulatory decisions. Whether particular mining actors exercise such control must be established through evidence.

D — Discretion: the latitude officials have to approve, investigate, enforce, delay, or decline intervention.

A — Accountability: the oversight, transparency, and consequences that hold those exercising power responsible for their decisions.

Klitgaard's formula is a diagnostic framework, not a numerical equation that proves corruption. It directs attention to who controls resources, who has the authority to intervene, how that authority is exercised, and what happens when oversight fails. The investigation tests these relationships rather than presuming that individual officials are corrupt.

The evidence presented in this investigation comes from different sources and periods. Figures from 2015 to 2017 refer to people presumed to be victims of human trafficking for various purposes, while court and prosecutorial records from 2017 to 2025 document individual cases. Some findings also draw on OjoPúblico, an independent Peruvian investigative journalism outlet, whose reporting examined 21 police reports.

The maps require similar care. Areas marked as mining activity are based on satellite analysis of changes to the landscape. These estimates help identify the extent of environmental disturbance, but they cannot establish whether every mining operation is illegal. Locations associated with trafficking investigations are approximate, and no private addresses of survivors are disclosed.

Throughout the investigation, allegations, documented rescue operations, disciplinary sanctions, and criminal convictions are treated separately. The purpose is to follow the evidence while remaining clear about what has been established and what still requires investigation.

HOW TO READ THESE SYMBOLS

Dots are not all connected: white outlined intersection rings mean multiple records refer to the same named locality, not that crimes were committed by the same people. Pink rings are aggregate historical location counts, not survivor addresses. White dots are geographic orientation points, not allegations. Pink connects documented case endpoints; blue connects records about the same official. Pale dashed lines denote optional geographic context only, and the pink dotted line links Puerto Maldonado recruitment with the broad La Pampa destination in one court-documented case. Click any dot to see what it represents. Gold outlines derive from a model-detected regional mining-scar raster and are generalized for display, with no color fill. The raster may overestimate mining footprints and does not establish illegality; legal status must be checked against permits, prohibited zones and government enforcement records. A separate thin blue outline marks the Madre de Dios region. Its geometry approximates the departmental boundary and provides geographic context, not evidence about any particular mine, settlement, or court case.
